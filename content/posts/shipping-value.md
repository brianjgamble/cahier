Instead of releasing multiple features, what if the focus was on shipping one thing? Does simplifying a feature or removing a complex one deliver value? Is the goal to always ship something new or to build software that improves the user experience?

## Good enough is not enough

Imagine the following.

The application you’re working on is a week away from release and you’re sitting in a go/no-go meeting with the rest of the team. The discussion centers around defects that would prevent a launch to production.

Eyes are focused on the front of the room where a projector displays the outstanding defect list. Defects are sorted from critical to low or 1 to 4. Talk centers on 1s and 2s; comments are abrupt and strained. The atmosphere is anything but calm.

_That’s a lot of bugs to fix before launch_, you think to yourself.

An argument breaks out between developers and product management over a handful of bugs marked critical. The noise in the room slowly rises until it reaches an awful din. Eventually, all sides agree to fix only the critical defects and the product ships.

The software is released _on time_, but are the users happy?

## Focus on the why

High-quality requirements are just as important as high-quality code. Each new requirement should delineate the problem solved and the user benefit. It’s easy to get caught up in the process of building software and forget to question why.

Development teams track burn-down, velocity, and defect rates. Are they measuring how the software is improving with each release?

## Deliver less

There’s a tension in development that drives a team to deliver new features; a mindset that believes it’s the only reason to ship. If new features are built on top of known bugs, can the software accommodate changes and evolve?

If the goal is shipping robust software and satisfied users, sometimes it means shipping nothing new.
